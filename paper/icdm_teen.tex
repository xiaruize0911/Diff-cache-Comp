\begin{abstract}
Step caching accelerates diffusion transformers by reusing a block residual
across neighbouring solver steps. The resulting speedup is training-free, but
the image degrades. A learned corrector can predict that reuse error; the
standard way to train it is residual regression. We show that this objective is
misaligned with deployed image quality, and we replace it with a
\emph{trajectory objective} that is strictly stronger at inference and costs
about twenty minutes of training.

Cache error is integrated along the sampling trajectory: an anchor refreshes
the cache, not the latent, so a per-step residual target is defined on a
trajectory the deployed corrector never visits. Training the same architecture
against latent-trajectory deviation, by backpropagating through the cached
rollout, improves image quality at every injection granularity we tested
($K{=}1$ to $28$, all significant, $t{=}2.7$ to $7.0$, $n{=}72$).
At a fixed step count on PixArt-$\Sigma$-XL-2-512 the trajectory-trained
corrector reaches SSIM $0.6820$ against the exact output at $3.41\times$
speedup. It outperforms a reproduction of TaylorSeer's extrapolation mechanism
by $+0.0946$ SSIM (\mbox{$t{=}14.5$}, $72$ of $72$ paired cases) at an $8.4\%$
speedup cost, and a reproduction of Block Caching by $+0.0490$ ($t{=}8.4$)
while running $12\%$ faster. Under ImageReward the separation is larger and one
verdict reverses: both reproduced mechanisms score \emph{below} verbatim
caching, while learned correction is the only method that improves on it.
The measurement that motivated the change is equally sharp: driving residual
accuracy $25$--$43\%$ better by matched-data retraining buys only $+0.005$ to
$+0.013$ SSIM, so residual error is a poor guide to what a corrector is worth.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Sampling a diffusion transformer \cite{peebles2023dit} costs one full stack
forward pass per solver step. Step caching exploits the smoothness of that
trajectory: an
\emph{anchor} step stores each block's residual $R=\mathrm{block}(h)-h$, and
the next $i-1$ \emph{reuse} steps skip the block and add the stored residual
instead \cite{ma2024deepcache,selvaraju2024fora,chen2024deltadit}. The method
is training-free and fast, and it visibly degrades the image.

Two families of remedies exist. Training-free extrapolation forecasts residual
drift from its own history \cite{liu2024teacache,liu2025taylorseer}. Learned
correction trains a small network \cite{wimbauer2024blockcaching,ma2024l2c} to
predict the reuse error
\begin{equation}
\dR \;=\; R_{\text{true}} - R_{\text{anchor}},
\label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
which is exactly the error verbatim reuse commits. This paper studies the
second family. Its default training signal is supervised regression on
\eqref{eq:target}, and every subsequent design choice --- architecture,
checkpoint selection, and how much correction to inject --- is then made in
residual space.

We show that this signal is misaligned with deployed quality, and that a
cheap replacement exists. Cache error is integrated along the sampling
trajectory, while residual error is pointwise; pushing residual accuracy
harder therefore buys almost nothing in image quality. We keep the
architecture, the cache schedule and the deployment rule, and train the same
corrector against the deviation of the latent trajectory by differentiating
through the cached rollout. Because a cached block never executes, that graph
is cheap. The resulting corrector improves image quality at every injection
granularity we tested, and at a fixed step count it outperforms reproductions
of two published mechanisms on three fidelity metrics and on a preference
model.

Concretely, we contribute:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A diagnosis of the residual objective.} The cached trajectory's
deviation from the exact one does not reset at cache refreshes: it climbs
almost monotonically ($0.0089$ at step~1 to $0.6092$ at step~19). An anchor
refreshes the cache, not the latent, so error already injected into the state
is permanent. Residual error is pointwise; trajectory error integrates.
\item \textbf{Evidence that residual accuracy is a poor investment.} Matching
training-data volume across granularities and training every rung to
convergence improves residual error by $25$--$43\%$ and image SSIM by only
$+0.005$ to $+0.013$, against $+0.033$ for the decision to add correction at
all.
\item \textbf{A trajectory objective that is cheap for a caching-specific
reason.} We backpropagate through the cached rollout. A cached block never
runs, so the graph over one reuse step is $K$ corrector forwards rather than
$K$ transformer blocks of a $0.6$B model. The change costs about $20$ minutes
of training and no architectural modification. It improves image quality at
every $K$, and by the largest margin where per-site residual training degrades
most.
\item \textbf{A same-step win against published mechanisms.} Holding the step
count and skip schedule fixed, the trajectory-trained corrector outperforms
reproductions of TaylorSeer and Block Caching on SSIM, PSNR and ImageReward,
at an $8.4\%$ speedup cost against the former and a speedup \emph{advantage}
against the latter. Under ImageReward, both reproduced mechanisms score below
verbatim caching; learned correction is the only method that improves on it.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph*{Fewer steps versus cheaper steps}
Latent diffusion \cite{rombach2022ldm} and DiT \cite{peebles2023dit} made
iterative sampling the default; PixArt-$\Sigma$ \cite{chen2024pixartsigma}
and rectified-flow transformers \cite{esser2024sd3,lipman2023flowmatching}
keep the same cost structure. Two orthogonal routes reduce it. High-order
ODE solvers \cite{song2020ddim,lu2022dpmsolver,lu2022dpmsolverpp,zhao2023unipc}
shorten the trajectory without retraining; distillation and consistency
training \cite{salimans2022progressive,song2023consistency,luo2023lcm} cut
it further, at the cost of a student. Caching instead makes each remaining
step cheaper, so the two compose. We hold the step count and the solver
fixed, which isolates the caching question but also means our comparisons
do not rank the two routes --- see Limitations.

\paragraph*{Training-free feature caching}
DeepCache \cite{ma2024deepcache} reuses U-Net skip-branch features across
steps. On transformers the natural unit is a block residual: FORA
\cite{selvaraju2024fora} caches attention and MLP outputs on a fixed
interval; $\Delta$-DiT \cite{chen2024deltadit} caches block-level offsets
with a front/back-loaded schedule; TeaCache \cite{liu2024teacache} modulates
the reuse interval from timestep-embedding distances; ToCa
\cite{zou2024toca} makes the decision per token; FasterCache
\cite{lv2024fastercache} exploits CFG redundancy in the frequency domain;
AdaCache \cite{kahatapitiya2024adacache} adapts the schedule to video
content. All of these decide \emph{when} to skip and then reuse a stale
quantity verbatim. Our correction-free baseline is uniform whole-stack step
caching, i.e.\ FORA at the coarsest granularity. We take the schedule as
given and ask what to add back.

\paragraph*{Learned skip patterns and learned corrections}
Learning-to-Cache \cite{ma2024l2c} learns which layers may be skipped.
LazyDiT \cite{shen2025lazydit} places a linear predictor in front of each
block and reuses from a first-order Taylor residual. Block Caching
\cite{wimbauer2024blockcaching} fits a timestep-conditioned per-channel
scale-shift --- trained, but a function of the timestep alone. TaylorSeer
\cite{liu2025taylorseer} replaces verbatim reuse with a training-free
finite-difference forecast of the cached residual. Our corrector differs
from all four in reading the \emph{current state}. That is what makes error
propagation across injection sites multiplicative
(Sec.~\ref{sec:granularity}), and it is why the training objective matters:
a state-independent correction cannot be improved by observing the
trajectory it produces.

\paragraph*{Objective mismatch and closed-loop training}
HarmoniCa \cite{huang2025harmonica} already names an ``objective mismatch''
between aligning predicted noise and generating good images, and fixes it
with an image-error-proxy objective and step-wise denoising training. We do
not claim the mismatch itself as novel. What we add is a cheaper and more
local diagnosis --- residual error is pointwise, cache error integrates
along the latent trajectory --- and a fix that does not require an
image-space proxy: the cached rollout is already differentiable, so the
trajectory deviation can be the loss. P2 \cite{choi2022p2} and Min-SNR
\cite{hang2023minsnr} reweight the same residual target so that no noise
level dominates; they do not replace it. DAgger \cite{ross2011dagger} and
scheduled sampling \cite{bengio2015scheduled} correct the data
distribution of a closed-loop policy. Here the distribution is not the
binding constraint --- the objective is --- and because a cached block never
executes, backpropagating through the rollout is cheap enough to skip the
resampling detour.

\section{Setup}

\paragraph*{Backbone and schedule}
PixArt-$\Sigma$-XL-2-512 \cite{chen2024pixartsigma} ($0.6$B parameters, $28$
blocks) with its shipped DPM-Solver++ scheduler \cite{lu2022dpmsolverpp} at $20$
steps and $512^2$ resolution. Anchors are uniform at interval $i{=}5$, so $4$
anchor steps and $16$ reuse steps. All numbers are against the same-seed exact
$20$-step output.

\paragraph*{Corrector}
A conditioned MLP ($11.9$M parameters) reads the state drift
$h_t-h_{\text{anchor}}$, the cached residual, and the anchor hidden state,
conditioned on the diffusion timestep, the reuse horizon $\tau$, and the site
index. At deployment
\begin{equation}
h_{\text{out}} \;=\; h_t + R_{\text{anchor}} + \sigma\,\widehat{\dR},
\label{eq:deploy}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ is an injection strength selected on validation and never on
test. The state-drift input is what makes the correction state-dependent,
which matters in Sec.~\ref{sec:granularity}. The $28$ blocks are grouped into
$K$ contiguous cached segments, so $K$ is the number of injection sites per
reuse step: $K{=}1$ corrects the whole stack once and $K{=}28$ corrects every
block.

\paragraph*{Corrector architecture}
Four residual MLP blocks of width $512$, AdaLN-modulated, with one
token-mixing attention layer ($4$ heads) and a rank-$256$ per-site linear
path, so the parameter count scales with $K$ ($11.9$M at $K{=}1$, $43.7$M
at $K{=}28$). Inputs are divided by their dataset RMS and the output
multiplied by the target RMS; without this the three inputs differ in scale
by up to $170\times$ and the state-drift signal is swamped.

\paragraph*{Supervision}
Feature collection runs the exact model and records, at every reuse step and
every site, the drift, the cached residual, the anchor hidden state, and the
target \eqref{eq:target}, subsampling $48$ of the $1024$ spatial tokens per
site to bound memory. Anchors are at steps $0,5,10,15$, so the horizon $\tau$
spans $1$ to $4$ and the target RMS grows $2.4\times$ across that range ---
which is why a pooled squared loss concentrates capacity on high $\tau$
whether or not that is intended. For the residual objective we match slot
count across granularities at exactly $50{,}176$ per rung using a seed
multiplier of $28/K$, and select the step budget per rung on validation.

\paragraph*{Trajectory-objective training}
AdamW, learning rate $10^{-5}$, gradient-norm clip $1.0$, $400$ steps, one
prompt per step, initialised from the residual-trained corrector of the same
$K$. The corrector is held in fp32 against an fp16 transformer;
each call is gradient-checkpointed, which keeps $K{=}28$ tractable. Cost is
measured, not estimated: $2.57$\,TFLOP per anchor step, $0.041$\,TFLOP per
corrector call, and $2.51$\,TFLOP fixed per image.

\paragraph*{Protocol}
Every image-level number is a paired comparison over $24$ held-out prompts
$\times$ $3$ seeds ($n{=}72$), with $\sigma$ re-selected on validation for
each corrector over a common grid. We report SSIM \cite{wang2004ssim},
PSNR, LPIPS \cite{zhang2018lpips} and ImageReward \cite{xu2023imagereward},
together with $t$ statistics and the number of cases that improve. Prompts
are templated ($10$ styles $\times$ $24$ subjects $\times$ $10$ conditions);
the held-out split shares no subject with training.

\section{Residual accuracy is a poor training signal}
\label{sec:diagnosis}

\paragraph*{Trajectory error integrates}
At each of the $20$ steps we measure
$\|x_{\text{cached}}-x_{\text{exact}}\|/\|x_{\text{exact}}\|$.
Deviation rises from $0.0089$ to $0.6092$, with only small dips at the four
anchors. This is the basic reason a per-step residual target is misaligned:
the corrector's action at step $t$ changes the state at $t{+}1$, while
\eqref{eq:target} is defined on the unperturbed trajectory.

\paragraph*{Better residual accuracy barely helps}
To rule out the possibility that the residual objective simply had not been
optimised hard enough, we rebuilt training from scratch: data volume matched
across granularities at exactly $50{,}176$ slots per rung (a seed multiplier
of $28/K$), and the step budget selected per rung on validation by doubling
until the gain fell below $0.5\%$. Residual error improved by $25$--$43\%$
depending on $K$. Image SSIM improved by $+0.0131$ at $K{=}1$, falling to
$+0.0055$ at $K{=}7$ --- all significant, all small. Two trends run opposite
along the granularity axis: the achievable residual improvement rises with
$K$ ($-27.7\%$ at $K{=}1$ to $-42.9\%$ at $K{=}28$) while the image quality it
buys falls.

\paragraph*{A ceiling on the residual form}
An oracle that recovers a fraction $\beta$ of the true residual has residual
error $(1-\beta)^2$. Final-step trajectory error falls from $0.6102$ at
$\beta{=}0$ to $0.4384$ at $\beta{=}0.8$, and to $0$ at $\beta{=}1$. The
curve is convex: the ceiling is perfect and the middle is flat, which rules
out the correction \emph{form} as the limit and locates the problem in the
objective. Trained correctors additionally beat an oracle at their own
equivalent $\beta$ by about $1.5\times$, because a scalar oracle shrinks
error isotropically while a trained corrector learns which directions matter
to the trajectory. Residual error therefore distorts both ways: as an
objective it overstates the gain, as a capability measure it understates
the corrector.

\section{Training against the trajectory}

We keep the architecture, the schedule and the deployment rule
\eqref{eq:deploy}, and change only the loss. Let $x_1,\dots,x_T$ be the cached
latents and $x^\star_1,\dots,x^\star_T$ the exact ones under the same seed.
The objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} \;=\; \sum_{t \in \text{reuse}}
\frac{\|x_t - x^\star_t\|^2}{\|x^\star_t\|^2},
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
minimised by backpropagating through the cached rollout. Supervision is free:
the exact trajectory comes from the same forward pass that produces the
corrector's input, so no annotation is involved.

Two implementation facts make this practical, and both are specific to
caching. First, a cached block never runs its transformer block --- the
runtime returns $h_t + R_{\text{anchor}} + \sigma\widehat{\dR}$ --- so the
graph over one reuse step consists of $K$ corrector forwards, not $K$ blocks
of a $0.6$B model. Peak memory is $7.5$\,GB at $K{=}1$; checkpointing each
corrector call brings $K{=}14$ from over $44$\,GB down to $4.2$\,GB. Second,
gradients are cut at anchor steps, where the cache is refreshed from exact
values, so a corrector call can only influence later intervals through the
latent. Extending the graph across intervals improves \eqref{eq:loss} but does
not improve image metrics, so we keep the one-interval truncation. Training is
$400$ steps, about $20$ minutes on one A40.

and the Block Caching mechanism \cite{wimbauer2024blockcaching} (a

not replications of the published results.

Paired against the trajectory-objective corrector, the first-order TaylorSeer
mechanism loses $0.0946$ SSIM ($t{=}14.5$) and $0.1197$ LPIPS ($t{=}14.2$),
with our corrector better on all $72$ of $72$ cases; the second-order version

$+0.0946$ SSIM, and against Block Caching we are both faster and better.
Residual-trained correction already outperforms both reproduced mechanisms;
the trajectory objective widens that lead.

The ImageReward column is not redundant with the fidelity metrics --- it
reverses one of their verdicts. Both reproduced mechanisms score \emph{below}
verbatim caching, by $-0.159$ and $-0.155$ respectively, each with $t{=}{-}2.1$.
Block Caching in particular looks harmless on SSIM ($+0.0060$) yet is
measurably worse than not correcting at all once a preference model scores the
image. Against it our corrector gains $+0.492$ ($t{=}6.3$, $60/72$), and
against first-order TaylorSeer $+0.497$ ($t{=}6.0$, $60/72$) --- larger
separations, relative to the metric's spread, than any of the fidelity metrics
give. Both learned-correction arms are the only entries that improve on
verbatim caching under a preference model ($+0.325$ and $+0.338$, $t{=}5.0$
and $4.2$).

Two caveats apply. TaylorSeer at $i{=}5$ is \emph{worse than no correction at
all} ($-0.0395$, only $3$ of $72$ cases improve); in our reproduction it helps
at $i{=}2$--$3$ and is past its useful extrapolation range by $i{=}5$, so
Table~\ref{tab:main} is not evidence against it at its own operating point.
And TaylorSeer is training-free, while our corrector costs training time,
$11.9$M parameters and $0.041$\,TFLOP per correction --- which is exactly
where the $8.4\%$ goes.

at all six granularities, every difference significant. The margin is largest
where per-site residual training degrades worst: $+0.0370$ at $K{=}7$ against
$+0.0147$ at $K{=}1$. This is consistent with the mechanism behind the
degradation. A state-dependent correction turns error propagation across sites
multiplicative rather than additive ---
$\delta_{b+1}\approx(I+J_b)\delta_b+\epsilon$ with
$J_b=\partial\widehat{\dR}/\partial h$ --- and the trajectory objective is
the only one that observes the consequence of that compounding rather than
pointwise error. The degradation is weakened, not removed: the residual
objective's gain falls $3$--$4\times$ from $K{=}1$ to high $K$, while the
trajectory objective's falls $1.2$--$1.8\times$.

$\sigma\in[0.18,0.25]$ while $K{=}14$ and $K{=}28$ are already negative at
$\sigma{=}0.25$. Both corrector generations trace the same family of curves,
so this is a property of granularity rather than of the objective.

\section{Scope and Limitations}

\paragraph*{Why fixed step count}
On this backbone ImageReward is already saturated by $10$ solver steps
($102\%$ of a $100$-step reference) while pixel fidelity keeps improving, so
an equal-compute comparison here mostly measures how early the shipped
high-order solver converges rather than how good a cache corrector is.
Holding the step count fixed isolates the question we are asking. Whether the
same trade-off holds on a backbone that needs many steps is open.

\paragraph*{Metric choice}
We report three fidelity metrics and a preference model rather than one
number, because they do not always agree: an objective trained on LPIPS wins
on LPIPS and gives up SSIM and PSNR. The trajectory objective is the arm that
wins on SSIM, PSNR and ImageReward simultaneously, which is why we report it
as the main result.

\paragraph*{Scope of the evidence}
Results are on $512^2$ PixArt-$\Sigma$ with templated prompts, $24$ held-out
prompts $\times$ $3$ seeds. Every reported gain is measured after only $400$
optimiser steps without hyperparameter search, starting from a
residual-trained corrector, so the numbers are conservative: they are what the
objective change buys on top of an already-trained model, not the best the
objective can do. One practical note for reimplementation: a corrector trained
to low residual error can overflow fp16 at inference, so the high-$K$
correctors are deployed in fp32.

\section{Conclusion}

The natural objective for a learned cache corrector --- regress the residual
it should have produced --- is misaligned with what caching actually damages.
Cache error integrates along the sampling trajectory; residual error is
pointwise. A $25$--$43\%$ residual improvement buys only $+0.005$ to
$+0.013$ SSIM. Replacing the objective with the trajectory deviation, which
costs $20$ minutes of training and no architectural change, improves image
quality at every injection granularity we tested. At a fixed step count the
resulting corrector outperforms reproductions of two published mechanisms on
three fidelity metrics and on a preference model, and is the only method in
that comparison that improves on verbatim caching under the preference model.
