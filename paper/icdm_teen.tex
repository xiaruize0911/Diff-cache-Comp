\begin{abstract}
Step caching accelerates diffusion transformers by reusing block residuals
across solver steps, at a cost in image quality. A learned corrector can
predict the reuse error; the natural way to train it is residual regression. We show that this objective is misaligned with
deployed quality: an anchor refreshes the cache but not the latent, so cache
error integrates along the trajectory while residual error is pointwise.
Retraining the residual corrector on more data to convergence lowers residual error by $25$--$30\%$ yet changes SSIM by only $-0.002$ to $+0.012$. We therefore fine-tune the same corrector with
\ourmethod{} (\emph{pathwise adjoint correction}), which minimises latent trajectory
deviation by differentiating through the cached rollout. On
held-out prompts, \ourmethod{} improves SSIM over the residual-trained corrector at every injection granularity we tested ($K{=}1$ to $28$ correction sites per step, paired $t{=}4.5$ to $10.0$, $n{=}72$) and LPIPS at every $K{\ge}2$. At a fixed step count on
PixArt-$\Sigma$-XL-2-512 it reaches SSIM $0.532$ against the exact output at
$3.39\times$ speedup, $+0.098$ above a reproduction of TaylorSeer
($t{=}12.8$) and $+0.053$ above one of Block Caching ($t{=}9.5$), at
$8\%$ and $3\%$ less speedup. Under
ImageReward, only state-dependent learned correction improves on verbatim
caching, and among these the residual objective scores higher than \ourmethod{}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Sampling a diffusion transformer \cite{peebles2023dit} costs a full forward
pass per solver step. Step caching exploits how slowly block outputs change: an
\emph{anchor} step, taken every $i$ steps, stores each block's residual
$R=\mathrm{block}(h)-h$ for input $h$, and
the next $i-1$ \emph{reuse} steps skip the block and add the stored residual
instead \cite{ma2024deepcache,selvaraju2024fora,chen2024deltadit}. The method needs no training and is fast, but it visibly degrades the image.

Besides choosing \emph{when} to reuse \cite{liu2024teacache,kahatapitiya2024adacache}, two remedies exist. Training-free extrapolation forecasts the residual from its own history \cite{liu2025taylorseer}. Learned correction trains a model, from a timestep-conditioned scale and shift \cite{wimbauer2024blockcaching} to a network that reads the current state (ours), to predict the reuse error
\begin{equation}
\dR \;=\; R_{\text{true}} - R_{\text{anchor}},
\label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
where $R_{\text{true}}$ is the residual the block would produce on the
current hidden state and $R_{\text{anchor}}$ is the one stored at the most
recent anchor. $\dR$ is exactly the error verbatim reuse commits. This paper studies the
second family. Its default training signal is supervised regression on
\eqref{eq:target}, and every later design choice (architecture, checkpoint selection, and how much correction to inject) is then made in residual space.

We show that this signal is misaligned with deployed quality, and that a cheap second training stage recovers much of what it misses. Concretely, we contribute:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A diagnosis of the residual objective.} The cached trajectory's
deviation from the exact one climbs almost monotonically ($0.0089$ at
step~1 to $0.5856$ at step~19): an anchor refreshes the cache, not the
latent. Retraining
with matched data lowers residual error by $25$--$30\%$ but changes SSIM by
only $-0.002$ to $+0.012$.
\item \textbf{\ourmethod{}.} The corrector can act once per step or at each of
$K$ groups of blocks. Keeping the architecture, schedule and deployment rule,
we fine-tune the corrector by differentiating through the cached rollout. A
cached block never runs, so the graph over one reuse step is $K$ corrector forwards rather than the $28$ blocks of the $0.6$B model. It improves SSIM at every $K$, with a $2$--$3\times$ larger margin at $K{\ge}4$, where residual training degrades most.
\item \textbf{A comparison with published mechanisms.} At a fixed step count and
skip schedule, \ourmethod{} outperforms reproductions of TaylorSeer and Block
Caching on SSIM, PSNR, LPIPS and ImageReward.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

Faster solvers \cite{song2020ddim,lu2022dpmsolverpp} and distillation
\cite{song2023consistency} shorten the sampling trajectory; caching makes each
remaining step cheaper. We hold the step count and solver fixed, which isolates
caching but does not rank the two routes.

\subsection{Training-Free Feature Caching}
DeepCache \cite{ma2024deepcache} reuses U-Net features; FORA
\cite{selvaraju2024fora} reuses transformer block residuals on a fixed interval
and $\Delta$-DiT \cite{chen2024deltadit} caches different blocks early and late.
TeaCache \cite{liu2024teacache}, ToCa \cite{zou2024toca}, FasterCache
\cite{lv2024fastercache} and AdaCache \cite{kahatapitiya2024adacache} adapt
\emph{when} to skip, then reuse verbatim. We take a uniform schedule as given
and ask what to add back.

\subsection{Learned Corrections and Training Objectives}
Learning-to-Cache \cite{ma2024l2c} learns which layers may be skipped.
LazyDiT \cite{shen2025lazydit} learns from each block's input when the block can be skipped and its cached output reused. Block Caching
\cite{wimbauer2024blockcaching} learns a per-channel scale and shift for each timestep: trained, but a function of the timestep alone. TaylorSeer
\cite{liu2025taylorseer} replaces verbatim reuse with a forecast of the cached residual from finite differences, with no training. Unlike all four, our corrector uses the \emph{current state} to correct the
reused residual. Its nonzero Jacobian makes errors compound across injection
sites (Sec.~\ref{sec:granularity}), which only a trajectory-level loss sees.
HarmoniCa \cite{huang2025harmonica} already names an ``objective mismatch''
and fixes it by training along the denoising process with a proxy for final
image error; we do not claim the mismatch as novel. We add a more local
diagnosis (residual error is pointwise, cache error integrates) and a fix with
no image-space proxy: the cached rollout is differentiable, so trajectory
deviation can be the loss. P2 \cite{choi2022p2} and Min-SNR
\cite{hang2023minsnr} reweight the residual target; they do not replace it.

\section{The Residual Objective}
\label{sec:diagnosis}

The measurements below use PixArt-$\Sigma$-XL-2-512
\cite{chen2024pixartsigma} ($0.6$B parameters, $28$ blocks) with its shipped
DPM-Solver++ scheduler \cite{lu2022dpmsolverpp} at $20$ steps and $512^2$.
Anchors are uniform at interval $i{=}5$ ($4$ anchor steps, $16$ reuse steps).
All image and trajectory numbers are against the exact $20$-step output from the same seed, on the
$24$ test prompts of Sec.~\ref{sec:experiments}. The $28$ blocks
are grouped into $K$ contiguous cached segments, so $K$ is the number of
injection sites per reuse step: $K{=}1$ corrects the whole stack once and
$K{=}28$ corrects every block.

\subsection{Trajectory Error}
At each of the $20$ steps we measure $\|x_t-x^\star_t\|/\|x^\star_t\|$, where
$x_t$ is the cached latent after step $t$ and $x^\star_t$ the exact one.
Averaged over the $24$ prompts, deviation rises from $0.0089$ to $0.5856$. It dips once, at the second anchor (step~5); later anchors only slow its growth.
This is the basic reason the residual target is misaligned: the corrector's
action at step $t$ changes the state at $t{+}1$, while \eqref{eq:target} is
defined on the unperturbed trajectory and residual regression
(Sec.~\ref{sec:method}) never differentiates through the reuse step.

\subsection{Residual Retraining}
\label{sec:retrain}
To check that residual regression had not simply been trained too little, we
gave every $K$ the same $50{,}176$ training examples, one per (image, reuse
step, site), by sampling $28/K$ seeds per prompt. We subsampled $48$ of $1024$ tokens per site and trained each $K$ for the step budget at which a doubling search found the next doubling to improve residual error by under $0.5\%$. Residual correctors keep the checkpoint with the lowest residual error on validation-prompt features, where residual errors are also measured. Against correctors trained with one seed per prompt and a $16$k-step budget, residual error fell $25$--$30\%$ for $K{\le}7$, while test SSIM changed by $+0.012$,
$+0.012$, $-0.002$ and $+0.006$ at $K{=}1,2,4,7$ ($t{=}2.9$, $2.7$, $-0.5$,
$2.0$), against $+0.039$ for adding correction at all ($K{=}1$). Residual
accuracy is a poor guide to what a corrector is worth.

\subsection{Oracle Correction}
An oracle that recovers a fraction $\beta$ of the true residual has residual
error $(1-\beta)^2$. Final-step trajectory error falls from $0.586$ at
$\beta{=}0$ to $0.381$ at $\beta{=}0.8$, and to $0$ at $\beta{=}1$. The curve
falls slowly up to $\beta{=}0.8$ and steeply after it. Additive injection can in principle recover the exact output ($\beta{=}1$); partial residual accuracy simply buys little. Each residual-trained corrector lowers final error $0.9$--$1.1\times$ as much
as an oracle with its residual error, but a better residual fit barely moves it:
the $27\%$ residual gain of matched-data retraining at $K{=}1$ maps to
$0.515\to0.492$ on the curve, yet the real correctors move $0.505\to0.502$. \ourmethod{} reaches $0.462$, close to an oracle that recovers $60\%$ of the residual ($0.457$).

\subsection{Step Caching}
A DiT block $b$ is residual,
$R_b(h)=\mathrm{block}_b(h)-h$, where $h$ is its input hidden state
($1024$ tokens $\times$ $1152$ channels), so one exact block maps
$h\mapsto h+R_b(h)$.
Let $t=0,\dots,T{-}1$ index solver steps ($T{=}20$) and $\mathcal{A}$ be the
anchor set (here $\{0,5,10,15\}$ at interval $i{=}5$).
On $t\in\mathcal{A}$ every block runs and the residual is stored. On a reuse
step the stored residual is added instead:
\begin{equation}
h \;\mapsto\; h + R_b(h_a),\qquad
a=\max\{s\in\mathcal{A}:s\le t\},
\label{eq:reuse}
\end{equation}
where $a$ is the latest anchor and $h_a$ the input the block saw there. With
blocks grouped into $K$ sites, the reuse error at site $k$ of step $t$ is
$\dR_{t,k}=R_{t,k}^{\mathrm{true}}-R_{t,k}(h_a)$, i.e.\
\eqref{eq:target}. The residual objective regresses the corrector's
prediction $\widehat{\dR}_{t,k}$ onto it on the
\emph{unperturbed} hidden states of the exact trajectory,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{res}}
\;=\;
\sum_{t\notin\mathcal{A}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}
\|\dR_{t,k}-\widehat{\dR}_{t,k}\|^2,
\label{eq:resloss}
\end{equation}
a mean squared error with targets divided by their global RMS.
No derivative is taken through \eqref{eq:reuse}, so \eqref{eq:resloss} cannot
see that a correction at step $t$ changes the state at $t{+}1$.

\subsection{Corrector}
The $28$ blocks are partitioned into $K$ contiguous cached segments. A
shared network $C_\theta$ is applied at each site. At a site it reads the state drift $\Delta h=h_t-h_a$, the residual $R_a$ the site's blocks produced at the anchor, and the anchor hidden state $h_a$, conditioned on the step $t$ (through its diffusion timestep), the reuse horizon
$\tau=t-a$ and the site index $k$. Each input is divided by its RMS over the
training set ($s_{\Delta h}$, $s_R$, $s_h$) and the head is
rescaled by the target RMS $s_{\dR}$,
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\dR}
\;=\;
s_{\dR}\,
C_\theta\!\left(
\frac{\Delta h}{s_{\Delta h}},\,
\frac{R_a}{s_R},\,
\frac{h_a}{s_h}
\;\middle|\; t,\tau,k
\right),
\label{eq:corrector}
\end{equation}
without which the three inputs differ in scale by up to about $180\times$.
A conditioning vector $c=\mathrm{MLP}([\gamma(t);\gamma(\tau);e_k])$ (Fourier
features $\gamma$, site embedding $e_k$) modulates, through AdaLN, a trunk of
four residual MLP blocks of width $512$, one $4$-head attention layer across
tokens, and a rank-$256$ linear path per site. The output projection is
zero-initialised, so $C_\theta{=}0$ is verbatim reuse ($11.9$M parameters at
$K{=}1$, $43.7$M at $K{=}28$). The site's output is
\begin{equation}
h_{\mathrm{out}}
\;=\;
h_t + R_a + \sigma\,\widehat{\dR},
\label{eq:deploy}
\end{equation}
with injection strength $\sigma$ selected on validation prompts, never on test prompts.
The Jacobian $J_k=\partial\widehat{\dR}_k/\partial h$ is nonzero because
$C_\theta$ reads $\Delta h$; that is what turns error across sites
multiplicative (Sec.~\ref{sec:granularity}).

\subsection{Pathwise Adjoint Training}
\ourmethod{} keeps \eqref{eq:corrector}--\eqref{eq:deploy} and replaces
\eqref{eq:resloss}. Let $x_t$ be the latent after step $t$ of the cached
rollout under \eqref{eq:deploy} and $x^\star_t$ the exact latent under the
same prompt and seed. The pathwise objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}
\;=\;
\sum_{t\notin\mathcal{A}}
\frac{\|x_t-x^\star_t\|^2}{\|x^\star_t\|^2},
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
minimised by reverse-mode differentiation through the cached rollout, which is the discrete adjoint of the sampler restricted to reuse steps
(Fig.~\ref{fig:method}b). No labels are needed: $x^\star$ is the exact rollout for the same prompt and seed, computed once without gradients.

The adjoint is cheap for a reason specific to caching. A cached block never
runs $\mathrm{block}_b$; the runtime returns \eqref{eq:deploy}, so the
graph over one reuse step is $K$ applications of $C_\theta$, not the $28$ blocks of a $0.6$B DiT. Peak memory is $7.5$\,GB at $K{=}1$ and $12.8$\,GB at
$K{=}2$; checkpointing each corrector call keeps $K{\ge}4$ at $3$--$6$\,GB. Gradients
are cut at anchors, where every block runs again on the current state, so a corrector call influences later intervals only through the latent.

\ourmethod{} is a second training stage, not a replacement trained from scratch:
residual regression is a cheap, teacher-forced way to reach a corrector that
already helps, just the wrong \emph{final} objective. We initialise from the
matched-data residual corrector of the same $K$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:retrain}), which
is also the ``Residual obj.'' arm below, so each comparison isolates the second
stage; training from zero initialisation is untested.
Training uses AdamW, learning rate $10^{-5}$, gradients clipped to norm $1.0$,
$400$ steps, one prompt per step, with $\sigma$ fixed during training ($0.75$ at $K{=}1$, $0.3$ at $K{=}2,4$, $0.2$ above). We keep the checkpoint with the lowest trajectory error on $12$ training prompts held back from the $48$ used for updates. $C_\theta$ is trained in fp32 against an fp16 transformer; at inference it runs in fp16 at $K{=}1$ and fp32 at $K{\ge}2$. Measured cost: $2.57$\,TFLOP per anchor step, $0.041$\,TFLOP per corrector call, $2.66$\,TFLOP fixed per image. One run
takes $23$ minutes at $K{=}1$ on an A40 shared with other jobs, and
$43$--$90$ minutes with checkpointing at $K{\ge}4$.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

Prompts are templated from $12$ styles, $39$ subjects and $12$ conditions,
and split by subject into $112$ training, $24$ validation and $24$ test
prompts ($27$, $6$ and $6$ subjects). Every
corrector's $\sigma$ is selected on validation $\times$ $3$ seeds over one
grid of $13$ values shared by all correctors. Every reported image metric is
a paired comparison on the test prompts $\times$ the same $3$ seeds
($n{=}72$), which selection never sees. We report SSIM \cite{wang2004ssim},
PSNR, LPIPS \cite{zhang2018lpips} and ImageReward \cite{xu2023imagereward},
with paired $t$ statistics and the number of improved cases.

and the Block Caching mechanism \cite{wimbauer2024blockcaching} (a per-channel scale and shift for each timestep, trained but blind to the state).
Both are reimplemented in our runtime rather than run from the authors' code,

Table~\ref{tab:perk} and Fig.~\ref{fig:main} show \ourmethod{} ahead of the
residual objective at all six granularities, every difference significant.
The margin is smallest at $K{=}1$ ($+0.0175$) and two to three times larger at
$K{\ge}4$ ($+0.037$ to $+0.047$), where the residual objective's own gain has
fallen to $+0.006$--$0.020$. This is consistent with the mechanism behind the
degradation. A correction that reads the state makes error propagate across
sites multiplicatively rather than additively,
$\delta_{k+1}=(I+\sigma J_k)\delta_k+\varepsilon_k$, where $\delta_k$ is the
deviation of the hidden state from the exact path on entering site $k$,
$\varepsilon_k$ is the residual error committed there, and
$J_k=\partial\widehat{\dR}_k/\partial h$ comes from \eqref{eq:corrector}. Only
\ourmethod{} observes the result of that compounding; residual training sees
pointwise error. From $K{=}1$ to $K{\ge}7$ the residual objective's gain falls
$2.6$--$8.6\times$, \ourmethod{}'s by at most $1.35\times$. \ourmethod{} also
improves LPIPS at every $K{\ge}2$ ($t{=}2.7$ to $9.9$), though not at $K{=}1$.

Fig.~\ref{fig:main} (right) also shows why $\sigma$ must be selected per
corrector. Under both objectives the validation optimum moves from $0.6$ at
$K{=}1$ to $0.15$--$0.22$ at $K{\ge}4$, so this is a property of granularity
rather than of the objective. \ourmethod{}'s peak also narrows: at $K{=}14$ and
$28$ it falls from its maximum to zero or below within $0.04$--$0.07$ of
$\sigma$.

\subsection{Scope and Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

We hold the step count fixed because on this backbone ImageReward saturates
by $10$ solver steps, so an equal-compute comparison mostly measures solver
convergence. Results are
on $512^2$ PixArt-$\Sigma$ with templated prompts; the $72$ test cases come from $6$ subjects. With seeds averaged per prompt ($n{=}24$), every \ourmethod{}-over-residual SSIM gain keeps $t{>}3.1$, while the ImageReward difference and the frozen-schedule lead below fall to $t{\approx}2.1$. Averaged per subject ($n{=}6$, the unit of the split), \ourmethod{} still leads at every $K$, in $4$--$6$ of the $6$ subjects ($t{=}2.6$ to $6.2$).

\emph{Schedules.} \ourmethod{} is trained with uniform anchors, but
$C_\theta$ is conditioned on $\tau$ rather than $i$, and \eqref{eq:loss} holds
for any anchor set. Adaptive policies (TeaCache, AdaCache) read the corrected
state, so for a controlled test we froze, per test case, the schedule a
TeaCache-style rule (accumulated relative change, without its fitted
rescaling) picks on an uncorrected rollout, and replayed it for every arm. It refreshes $4.0$ times on
average, like the uniform schedule, but unevenly (e.g.\ anchors
$\{0,11,15,18\}$), so $\tau$ reaches $8$--$11$ against $4$ in training.
Table~\ref{tab:sched} gives the result. Without retraining, both $K{=}1$
correctors still help ($+0.020$ and $+0.027$ SSIM, $t{=}4.1$ and $5.1$) and
\ourmethod{} still leads ($+0.007$, $t{=}2.4$), but the gains shrink to about
$40\%$ of their uniform-schedule values. The schedule hurts verbatim caching
perceptually (LPIPS $0.537\to0.603$) while leaving its SSIM unchanged, and on
it \ourmethod{}'s lead extends to LPIPS ($0.024$ better, $t{=}5.7$) and PSNR
($+0.28$\,dB, $t{=}4.4$), neither of which it has under uniform anchors at
$K{=}1$. In LPIPS the residual objective loses more of its gain over verbatim caching
($0.100\to0.059$) than \ourmethod{} does ($0.094\to0.083$). Training on the
deployed schedule is the natural remedy and is untested.

\section{Conclusion}

Regressing the residual a cache corrector should have produced is misaligned
with what caching damages: cache error integrates along the trajectory, residual
error is pointwise. Fine-tuning the same corrector against trajectory deviation improves SSIM at every injection
granularity we tested and LPIPS at every $K{\ge}2$. At a fixed step count it
outperforms reproductions of two published mechanisms on all four metrics.
Under the preference model, state-dependent learned correction is the only
approach that improves on verbatim caching, with the residual objective ahead
of \ourmethod{} at $K{=}1$. Code, configurations, logs and per-case metrics
are at \url{https://github.com/xiaruize0911/Diff-cache-Comp};
