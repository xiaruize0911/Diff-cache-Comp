\begin{abstract}
Step caching accelerates diffusion transformers by reusing a block residual
across neighbouring solver steps. The speedup needs no training, but the image degrades. A learned corrector can predict that reuse error; the
standard way to train it is residual regression. We show that this objective is
misaligned with deployed image quality: cache error integrates along the
sampling trajectory, because an anchor refreshes the cache but not the latent,
so the residual target at each step is defined on a trajectory the deployed corrector never visits. Driving residual accuracy $25$--$43\%$ higher by retraining on matched data buys only $+0.005$ to $+0.013$ SSIM. We therefore fine-tune the same
corrector with \ourmethod{} (\emph{pathwise adjoint correction}), which
minimises the deviation of the latent trajectory by differentiating through the cached rollout, in about twenty minutes of training. \ourmethod{} improves image
quality at every injection granularity we tested
($K{=}1$ to $28$, all significant, $t{=}2.7$ to $7.0$, $n{=}72$).
At a fixed step count on PixArt-$\Sigma$-XL-2-512, \ourmethod{} reaches
SSIM $0.6820$ against the exact output at $3.41\times$
speedup. It outperforms a reproduction of TaylorSeer's extrapolation mechanism
by $+0.0946$ SSIM (\mbox{$t{=}14.5$}, $72$ of $72$ paired cases) at an $8.4\%$
speedup cost, and a reproduction of Block Caching by $+0.0490$ ($t{=}8.4$)
while running $12\%$ faster. Under ImageReward both reproduced mechanisms score
\emph{below} verbatim caching, and learned correction is the only method that
improves on it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Sampling a diffusion transformer \cite{peebles2023dit} costs one full stack
forward pass per solver step. Step caching exploits the smoothness of that
trajectory: an
\emph{anchor} step, taken every $i$ steps, stores each block's residual
$R=\mathrm{block}(h)-h$ for input $h$, and
the next $i-1$ \emph{reuse} steps skip the block and add the stored residual
instead \cite{ma2024deepcache,selvaraju2024fora,chen2024deltadit}. The method needs no training and is fast, but it visibly degrades the image.

Two families of remedies exist. Training-free extrapolation forecasts residual
drift from its own history \cite{liu2024teacache,liu2025taylorseer}. Learned
correction trains a small network \cite{wimbauer2024blockcaching,ma2024l2c} to
predict the reuse error
\begin{equation}
\dR \;=\; R_{\text{true}} - R_{\text{anchor}},
\label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
where $R_{\text{true}}$ is the residual the block would produce on the
current hidden state and $R_{\text{anchor}}$ is the one stored at the most
recent anchor. $\dR$ is exactly the error verbatim reuse commits. This paper studies the
second family. Its default training signal is supervised regression on
\eqref{eq:target}, and every later design choice (architecture, checkpoint selection, and how much correction to inject) is then made in residual space.

We show that this signal is misaligned with deployed quality, and that a
cheap second training stage fixes much of it. Concretely, we contribute:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A diagnosis of the residual objective.} The cached trajectory's
deviation from the exact one climbs almost monotonically ($0.0089$ at
step~1 to $0.6092$ at step~19): an anchor refreshes the cache, not the
latent. Residual error is pointwise; trajectory error integrates. Retraining
with matched data to convergence improves residual error by $25$--$43\%$ but
image SSIM by only $+0.005$ to $+0.013$.
\item \textbf{\ourmethod{}.} Keeping the architecture, schedule and
deployment rule, we fine-tune the corrector by differentiating through the
cached rollout. A cached block never runs, so the graph over one reuse step
is $K$ corrector forwards rather than $K$ blocks of a $0.6$B model; training
takes about $20$ minutes. It improves image quality at every $K$, and most where residual training degrades most.
\item \textbf{A comparison with published mechanisms.} At a fixed step count and skip schedule, \ourmethod{} outperforms reproductions of
TaylorSeer and Block Caching on SSIM, PSNR and ImageReward.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Fewer Steps Versus Cheaper Steps}
Latent diffusion \cite{rombach2022ldm} and DiT \cite{peebles2023dit} made
iterative sampling the default; PixArt-$\Sigma$ \cite{chen2024pixartsigma}
and rectified-flow transformers \cite{esser2024sd3,lipman2023flowmatching}
keep that cost structure. High-order ODE solvers
\cite{song2020ddim,lu2022dpmsolver,lu2022dpmsolverpp,zhao2023unipc} and
distillation \cite{salimans2022progressive,song2023consistency,luo2023lcm}
shorten the trajectory; caching makes each remaining step cheaper. We hold
the step count and solver fixed, which isolates caching but does not rank
the two routes (Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Training-Free Feature Caching}
DeepCache \cite{ma2024deepcache} reuses U-Net skip features. On
transformers the unit is a block residual: FORA \cite{selvaraju2024fora}
on a fixed interval; $\Delta$-DiT \cite{chen2024deltadit} caches different blocks early and late; TeaCache \cite{liu2024teacache}, ToCa
\cite{zou2024toca}, FasterCache \cite{lv2024fastercache} and AdaCache
\cite{kahatapitiya2024adacache} adapt \emph{when} to skip, then reuse
verbatim. Our baseline caches the whole stack at uniform intervals, with no correction. We take the schedule as given and ask what to add back.

\subsection{Learned Corrections and Training Objectives}
Learning-to-Cache \cite{ma2024l2c} learns which layers may be skipped.
LazyDiT \cite{shen2025lazydit} places a linear predictor in front of each
block and reuses from a first-order Taylor residual. Block Caching
\cite{wimbauer2024blockcaching} learns a per-channel scale and shift for each timestep: trained, but a function of the timestep alone. TaylorSeer
\cite{liu2025taylorseer} replaces verbatim reuse with a forecast of the cached residual from finite differences, with no training. Our corrector differs
from all four in reading the \emph{current state}. That is what makes error
propagation across injection sites multiplicative
(Sec.~\ref{sec:granularity}), and it is why the training objective matters:
a correction that ignores the state cannot be improved by observing the trajectory it produces.
HarmoniCa \cite{huang2025harmonica} already names an ``objective mismatch''
between aligning predicted noise and generating good images, and fixes it by training along the denoising process with a proxy for final image error in the objective. We do
not claim the mismatch itself as novel. What we add is a cheaper and more local diagnosis (residual error is pointwise, while cache error integrates along the latent trajectory) and a fix that needs no proxy in image space: the cached rollout is already differentiable, so the
trajectory deviation can be the loss. P2 \cite{choi2022p2} and Min-SNR
\cite{hang2023minsnr} reweight the residual target; they do not replace it.
DAgger \cite{ross2011dagger} and scheduled sampling
\cite{bengio2015scheduled} correct a closed-loop data distribution. Here the
binding constraint is the objective, and the cached rollout is already cheap
to differentiate, so the resampling detour is unnecessary.

\section{The Residual Objective}
\label{sec:diagnosis}

The measurements below use PixArt-$\Sigma$-XL-2-512
\cite{chen2024pixartsigma} ($0.6$B parameters, $28$ blocks) with its shipped
DPM-Solver++ scheduler \cite{lu2022dpmsolverpp} at $20$ steps and $512^2$.
Anchors are uniform at interval $i{=}5$ ($4$ anchor steps, $16$ reuse steps).
All numbers are against the exact $20$-step output from the same seed. The $28$ blocks
are grouped into $K$ contiguous cached segments, so $K$ is the number of
injection sites per reuse step: $K{=}1$ corrects the whole stack once and
$K{=}28$ corrects every block.

\subsection{Trajectory Error}
At each of the $20$ steps we measure
$\|x_{\text{cached}}-x_{\text{exact}}\|/\|x_{\text{exact}}\|$.
Deviation rises from $0.0089$ to $0.6092$, with only small dips at the four
anchors. This is the basic reason the residual target is misaligned:
the corrector's action at step $t$ changes the state at $t{+}1$, while
\eqref{eq:target} is defined on the unperturbed trajectory and
\eqref{eq:resloss} never differentiates through \eqref{eq:reuse}.

\subsection{Residual Retraining}
To check that \eqref{eq:resloss} had not simply been trained too little, we
matched data volume at $50{,}176$ slots per rung (seed multiplier $28/K$),
subsampled $48$ of $1024$ tokens per site, and doubled the step budget until
the gain fell below $0.5\%$. Residual error improved $25$--$43\%$. Image
SSIM improved by $+0.0131$ at $K{=}1$, to $+0.0055$ at $K{=}7$: significant, but small. Residual improvement rises with $K$ while the image
quality it buys falls.

\subsection{Oracle Correction}
An oracle that recovers a fraction $\beta$ of the true residual has residual
error $(1-\beta)^2$. Trajectory error at the last step falls from $0.6102$ at
$\beta{=}0$ to $0.4384$ at $\beta{=}0.8$, and to $0$ at $\beta{=}1$. The
curve is convex: the ceiling is perfect and the middle is flat, which rules
out the correction \emph{form} as the limit and locates the problem in the
objective. Trained correctors additionally beat an oracle at their own
equivalent $\beta$ by about $1.5\times$, because a scalar oracle shrinks
error isotropically while a trained corrector learns which directions matter
to the trajectory. Residual error therefore distorts both ways: as an
objective it overstates the gain, as a capability measure it understates
the corrector.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Step Caching}
A DiT block $b$ is residual,
$R_b(h)=\mathrm{block}_b(h)-h$, where $h$ is its input hidden state
($1024$ tokens $\times$ $1152$ channels), so one exact block maps
$h\mapsto h+R_b(h)$.
Let $t=0,\dots,T{-}1$ index solver steps ($T{=}20$) and $\mathcal{A}$ be the
anchor set (here $\{0,5,10,15\}$ at interval $i{=}5$).
On $t\in\mathcal{A}$ every block runs and the residual is stored. On a reuse
step the stored residual is added instead:
\begin{equation}
h \;\mapsto\; h + R_b(h_a),\qquad
a=\max\{s\in\mathcal{A}:s\le t\},
\label{eq:reuse}
\end{equation}
where $a$ is the latest anchor and $h_a$ the input the block saw there. With
blocks grouped into $K$ sites, the reuse error at site $k$ of step $t$ is
$\dR_{t,k}=R_{t,k}^{\mathrm{true}}-R_{t,k}(h_a)$, i.e.\
\eqref{eq:target}. The residual objective regresses the corrector's
prediction $\widehat{\dR}_{t,k}$ onto it on the
\emph{unperturbed} hidden states of the exact trajectory,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{res}}
\;=\;
\sum_{t\notin\mathcal{A}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}
\frac{\|\dR_{t,k}-\widehat{\dR}_{t,k}\|^2}{\|\dR_{t,k}\|^2}.
\label{eq:resloss}
\end{equation}
No derivative is taken through \eqref{eq:reuse}, so \eqref{eq:resloss} cannot
see that a correction at step $t$ changes the state at $t{+}1$.

\subsection{Corrector}
The $28$ blocks are partitioned into $K$ contiguous cached segments. A
shared network $C_\theta$ is applied at each site. It reads the state drift
$\Delta h=h_t-h_a$, the cached residual $R_a$ and the anchor hidden state
$h_a$, conditioned on the diffusion timestep $t$, the reuse horizon
$\tau=t-a$ and the site index $k$. Each input is divided by its RMS over the
training set ($s_{\Delta h}$, $s_R$, $s_h$) and the head is
rescaled by the target RMS $s_{\dR}$,
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\dR}
\;=\;
s_{\dR}\,
C_\theta\!\left(
\frac{\Delta h}{s_{\Delta h}},\,
\frac{R_a}{s_R},\,
\frac{h_a}{s_h}
\;\middle|\; t,\tau,k
\right),
\label{eq:corrector}
\end{equation}
without which the three inputs differ in scale by up to $170\times$.
Conditioning is $c=\mathrm{MLP}([\gamma(t);\gamma(\tau);e_k])$ with Fourier
features $\gamma$ and a site embedding $e_k$. The trunk is four AdaLN
residual MLP blocks of width $512$, one $4$-head attention layer across tokens, and a rank-$256$ linear path for each site. The output projection is
zero-initialised, so $C_\theta{=}0$ is verbatim reuse ($11.9$M parameters at
$K{=}1$, $43.7$M at $K{=}28$). Deployment is
\begin{equation}
h_{\mathrm{out}}
\;=\;
h_t + R_a + \sigma\,\widehat{\dR},
\label{eq:deploy}
\end{equation}
with injection strength $\sigma$ selected on validation, never on test.
The Jacobian $J_k=\partial\widehat{\dR}_k/\partial h$ is nonzero because
$C_\theta$ reads $\Delta h$; that is what turns error across sites
multiplicative (Sec.~\ref{sec:granularity}).

\subsection{Pathwise Adjoint Training}
\ourmethod{} keeps \eqref{eq:corrector}--\eqref{eq:deploy} and replaces
\eqref{eq:resloss}. Let $x_t$ be the latent after step $t$ of the cached
rollout under \eqref{eq:deploy} and $x^\star_t$ the exact latent under the
same prompt and seed. The pathwise objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}
\;=\;
\sum_{t\notin\mathcal{A}}
\frac{\|x_t-x^\star_t\|^2}{\|x^\star_t\|^2},
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
minimised by reverse-mode differentiation through the cached rollout, which is the discrete adjoint of the sampler restricted to reuse steps
(Fig.~\ref{fig:method}b). Supervision is free: $x^\star$ is produced by the
same forward pass that yields $C_\theta$'s input.

The adjoint is cheap for a reason specific to caching. A cached block never
runs $\mathrm{block}_b$; the runtime returns \eqref{eq:deploy}, so the
graph over one reuse step is $K$ applications of $C_\theta$, not $K$ blocks
of a $0.6$B DiT. Peak memory is $7.5$\,GB at $K{=}1$; checkpointing each
corrector call brings $K{=}14$ from over $44$\,GB to $4.2$\,GB. Gradients
are cut at anchors, where the cache is refreshed from exact values, so a
corrector call influences later intervals only through the latent.
Extending the graph across intervals improves \eqref{eq:loss} but not image
metrics; we keep the graph to one interval.

\ourmethod{} is a second training stage, not a replacement trained from scratch.
Residual regression is a cheap, teacher-forced way to reach a corrector that
already helps; we argue it is the wrong \emph{final} objective, not a useless
one. We therefore initialise from the residual corrector of the same $K$ retrained on matched data (Sec.~\ref{sec:diagnosis}), already trained until more steps stopped
helping; it is also the ``Residual obj.'' arm below, so each comparison
isolates the second stage. We did not test pathwise training from the
zero-initialised corrector.
Training uses AdamW, learning rate $10^{-5}$, gradients clipped to norm $1.0$,
$400$ steps, one prompt per step. $C_\theta$ is held in fp32 against an fp16
transformer. Cost is measured: $2.57$\,TFLOP per anchor step,
$0.041$\,TFLOP per corrector call, $2.51$\,TFLOP fixed per image. One run
is about $20$ minutes on an A40.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

Every image metric is a paired comparison over $24$ held-out prompts
$\times$ $3$ seeds ($n{=}72$), with $\sigma$ re-selected on validation for
each corrector over a common grid. We report SSIM \cite{wang2004ssim},
PSNR, LPIPS \cite{zhang2018lpips} and ImageReward \cite{xu2023imagereward},
together with $t$ statistics and the number of cases that improve. Prompts
are templated ($10$ styles $\times$ $24$ subjects $\times$ $10$ conditions);
the held-out split shares no subject with training.

at all six granularities, every difference significant. The margin is largest where residual training degrades worst: $+0.0370$ at $K{=}7$ against
$+0.0147$ at $K{=}1$. This is consistent with the mechanism behind the
degradation. A correction that reads the state makes error propagate across sites multiplicatively rather than additively, $\delta_{k+1}=(I+\sigma J_k)\delta_k+\varepsilon_k$, where $\delta_k$ is the deviation of the hidden state from the exact path on entering site $k$, $\varepsilon_k$ is the residual error committed there, and $J_k=\partial\widehat{\dR}_k/\partial h$ comes from \eqref{eq:corrector}. objective's gain falls $3$--$4\times$ from $K{=}1$ to high $K$, while

$\sigma{=}0.25$. Both corrector generations trace the same family of curves,

\subsection{Scope and Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

On this backbone ImageReward saturates by $10$ solver steps while pixel
fidelity keeps improving, so a comparison at equal compute here mostly measures solver convergence. Holding the step count fixed isolates caching.
Results are on $512^2$ PixArt-$\Sigma$ with templated prompts ($n{=}72$).

\emph{Schedules.} All results use uniform anchors. Nothing in the method
assumes them: $C_\theta$ is conditioned on $\tau$ rather than on $i$, and
\eqref{eq:loss} is defined for any anchor set $\mathcal{A}$, so \ourmethod{}
can be trained on the schedule it will be deployed with. Adaptive policies
such as TeaCache or AdaCache couple the two. Their refresh decision reads the
corrected state, so each corrector would choose its own schedule and compute
budget. In a pilot with earlier $K{=}1$ correctors trained on residuals, we therefore froze a schedule in the style of TeaCache (refresh when accumulated change crosses a threshold, without its fitted rescaling), chosen on a rollout with no correction
(e.g.\ anchors $\{0,11,15,18\}$, so $\tau$ reaches $11$ against $4$ in
training). Correction still helped
($+0.008$ and $+0.021$ SSIM for two training losses, $n{=}48$) but less than
under uniform anchors ($+0.036$ and $+0.047$). We have not trained or
tested \ourmethod{} on non-uniform schedules.
Gains are after $400$ optimiser steps without hyperparameter search, from a corrector trained on residuals, so they are what the objective change buys, not
an upper bound. High-$K$ correctors are deployed in fp32 to avoid fp16
overflow.

\section{Conclusion}

The natural objective for a learned cache corrector is to regress the residual it should have produced. That objective is misaligned with what caching damages: cache error integrates along the sampling trajectory, while residual error is pointwise.
Fine-tuning the same corrector against trajectory deviation, at a cost of
$20$ minutes and no architectural change, improves image quality at every
injection granularity we tested. At a fixed step count, learned
correction is the only approach that improves on verbatim caching under a
preference model, and \ourmethod{} leads on SSIM and PSNR.
