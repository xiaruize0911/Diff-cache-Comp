\begin{abstract}
Step caching accelerates diffusion transformers by reusing a block's output residual
across adjacent denoising steps. A natural refinement is to learn a \emph{corrector}
that predicts how the cached residual should change, and the natural way to train and
select such a corrector is to minimise its reconstruction error in residual space.
That this objective is imperfectly aligned with image quality is known
\cite{huang2025harmonica}. We show something stronger and more specific: it
\emph{inverts the ranking}. In a controlled experiment holding data, architecture,
injection structure and FLOPs fixed and varying only the training loss, residual
error prefers the worse model in all three training seeds per arm while held-out image
quality prefers the other in all three --- one clean rank separation, which we
deliberately do not inflate into nine independent comparisons. We identify the mechanism: residual error aggregates
squared error and target energy separately, so it is dominated by high-magnitude
injection sites; at deployment every site feeds the same depth-wise propagation chain
regardless of its magnitude, making \emph{per-site relative} error the
deployment-aligned statistic. Two consequences follow. First, making the training
objective per-site scale invariant --- a three-line change --- improves deployed
fidelity at matched data, architecture, parameter count and wall clock. In a design with no confound we can
identify --- three training seeds per arm, both arms stopped at the same step with no
checkpoint selection, one damping value per arm chosen on validation, $192$ held-out
images --- it is worth $+0.0077$ SSIM and $-0.0091$ LPIPS, and both survive clustering
by prompt subject ($t(5){=}2.74$ and $-3.18$; CIs $[+0.002,+0.015]$ and
$[-0.015,-0.003]$). The direction is positive in every condition and metric we
measured. The magnitude is small and we have repeatedly over-estimated it: successive
estimates as our designs got cleaner were $+0.022$, $+0.012$, $+0.027$ and now
$+0.008$. We therefore claim the \emph{ordering}, which is stable, and not the size. Second, a per-site median relative error ranks correctors in deployment order where
residual error does not --- reliably for correctors trained on identical data, and not
across training sets, so we offer it as a hypothesis rather than a validated
criterion. We further report a negative result that the same lens
predicts: on-policy (DAgger-style) data collection, which residual error credits with
an $11.9\%$ improvement, yields no measurable image-level benefit over an equal
volume of ordinary data. On a second, architecturally unrelated backbone
(FLUX.1-dev, 12B) the residual-space signature replicates exactly but the image-level
benefit of the fix does not; we give a quantitative account --- the effect should
scale with the per-site target-energy imbalance, which is $3\times$ smaller there ---
and state the resulting prediction for other backbones.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Diffusion samplers spend nearly all of their compute inside a transformer block
stack that is evaluated once per denoising step. Because adjacent steps compute
similar functions, a large family of training-free accelerators for diffusion transformers \cite{peebles2023dit}
\emph{caches} intermediate quantities and reuses them for several steps
\cite{ma2024deepcache,selvaraju2024fora,chen2024deltadit,liu2024teacache,zou2024toca}.
Concretely, if a block maps hidden state $h$ to $\mathrm{Block}(h)$, one caches the
residual $R = \mathrm{Block}(h) - h$ at an \emph{anchor} step and, at subsequent
\emph{reuse} steps, approximates the block by $h + R_{\text{anchor}}$ at zero cost.

The obvious way to push this further is to stop assuming the residual is constant.
Since the hidden state has drifted from the anchor, one can learn a corrector $C$
that predicts the residual's change,
\begin{equation}
\hat{\dR} = C\!\left(h - h_a,\, R_a,\, h_a,\, t,\, \tau\right),
\qquad
h_{\text{out}} \approx h + R_a + \hat{\dR},
\label{eq:corrector}
\end{equation}
where $h_a, R_a$ are the anchor state and residual, $t$ is the timestep and
$\tau$ the number of steps since the anchor. Training $C$ is a supervised regression
problem with free labels: the true residual at any state is obtained by running the
real block once. The natural objective, and the one we ourselves used initially, is
relative residual error
\begin{equation}
\relmse = \frac{\sum_i \lVert \dR_i - \hat{\dR}_i \rVert^2}{\sum_i \lVert \dR_i \rVert^2},
\label{eq:relmse}
\end{equation}
which equals $1$ for the ``predict nothing'' baseline, i.e.\ plain caching.

This paper argues that Eq.~\ref{eq:relmse} is the wrong objective, and that the error
is not a matter of degree. Our contributions:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{An inversion, not a gap.} With data, architecture, injection
structure and FLOPs held fixed and only the loss varied, $\relmse$ and held-out image
quality rank two correctors in \emph{opposite} orders, consistently across three
training seeds (Sec.~\ref{sec:inversion}). Residual error is stable to
$5\times10^{-4}$ across training seeds, an order of magnitude below the gap between
the arms, so the metric is reliably wrong rather than noisy. We report two variance
components throughout and never conflate them: the spread across training seeds
(small; SD $\le\!0.008$ SSIM) and the uncertainty from finite held-out data
(larger; the subject-clustered standard error of the \emph{contrast} is $0.012$ SSIM).
The first bounds how much a rerun would move a number; only the second bounds
generalisation, and with $6$ held-out subjects it is the binding one.

\item \textbf{A mechanism.} $\relmse$ sums squared error and target energy
separately, so sites whose $\lVert \dR \rVert$ is large dominate it. Deployment does
not share that weighting: each injection site feeds the same propagation chain
irrespective of magnitude (Sec.~\ref{sec:mechanism}). This also explains a
previously puzzling observation in this line of work --- that correcting a cached
stack \emph{per block} destroys the image while correcting it \emph{once} helps.

\item \textbf{A fix, and when it will not help.} A per-site scale-invariant loss
is worth $+0.008$ SSIM and $-0.009$ LPIPS at matched cost in our cleanest design
(Sec.~\ref{sec:loss}) --- small, but significant on both metrics under subject
clustering. Correction itself is worth several times more
($+0.034$--$+0.054$ SSIM over plain caching). Reweighting a
diffusion MSE so no scale dominates is not itself new
\cite{choi2022p2,hang2023minsnr}; what is new is identifying \emph{which} imbalance
matters for cache correction, that it inverts rather than blurs the ranking, and a
measurable predictor of when the fix pays --- the effect tracks the per-site
target-energy spread across three conditions (Sec.~\ref{sec:scaling}) and vanishes
when that spread is flat.

\item \textbf{A negative result the lens predicts.} On-policy data collection in the
style of DAgger \cite{ross2011dagger} improves $\relmse$ by $11.9\%$ and delivers
nothing on images; at matched data volume, ordinary data is if anything slightly
better (Sec.~\ref{sec:dagger}).

\item \textbf{Cross-backbone limits.} On FLUX.1-dev (12B, flow matching, 57
blocks) correction itself transfers strongly ($+0.0808$ SSIM over plain caching,
$t{=}10.8$) and the residual-space signature replicates exactly, but the loss fix
gives \emph{no} measurable image-level benefit. We trace this to a measurable
backbone property --- per-site target-energy imbalance, $10.9\times$ on our primary
backbone versus $3.7\times$ on FLUX --- which bounds how much the fix can buy
(Sec.~\ref{sec:flux}).
\end{itemize}

We stress the scope of the claim. We do not propose a new state-of-the-art
accelerator; the absolute gains from correction are real but modest
($\approx\!0.05$ SSIM at $4\times$ FLOPs reduction). The contribution is that the
standard way of building and evaluating such correctors is measuring the wrong thing,
by an amount large enough to reverse conclusions --- including, in our own case, a
conclusion we had already written down.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Caching and reuse.} DeepCache \cite{ma2024deepcache} reuses U-Net
skip-branch features across steps. For transformers, FORA
\cite{selvaraju2024fora} caches attention and MLP outputs on a fixed step interval;
$\Delta$-DiT \cite{chen2024deltadit} caches block-level offsets with a
front/back-loaded schedule; TeaCache \cite{liu2024teacache} modulates the reuse
interval using timestep-embedding distances; ToCa \cite{zou2024toca} makes the
decision per token. All are training-free and reuse a stale quantity verbatim. Our
correction-free baseline is uniform whole-stack step caching, i.e.\ the mechanism
underlying FORA at the coarsest granularity; our subject is what happens when one
adds a \emph{learned} correction on top of any such scheme, and how that corrector
should be trained and chosen.

\paragraph{Predicting the change instead of reusing it.} Eq.~\ref{eq:corrector} is
not a new idea. TaylorSeer \cite{liu2025taylorseer} replaces verbatim reuse with a
training-free Taylor extrapolation of how each cached feature evolves, and reports
substantially stronger absolute results than we do --- near-lossless generation at
$4.99\times$ compression on FLUX.1-dev. Block Caching
\cite{wimbauer2024blockcaching} fits lightweight scale--shift corrections to cached
block outputs, and Learning-to-Cache \cite{ma2024l2c} learns which layers may be
cached at all. \textbf{We are not competitive with these methods in absolute terms and
do not claim to be}; our operating points are chosen to make a diagnostic comparison
clean, not to win a speed/quality benchmark.

\paragraph{Our thesis is a sharpening of a known one.} That the training objective for
a learned cache mechanism is misaligned with image quality has been argued before, most
directly by HarmoniCa \cite{huang2025harmonica}, which names an ``objective mismatch''
between aligning predicted noise and generating good images and fixes it with an
image-error-proxy objective and step-wise denoising training. We therefore do not claim
the misalignment itself as novel. What we add is (i) that the mismatch is not a matter
of degree but reverses the ranking of two correctors, reproducibly and across metrics;
(ii) a specific and measurable mechanism for it --- per-injection-site target-energy
imbalance --- with a predictor of when it will and will not matter
(Sec.~\ref{sec:scaling}); (iii) a much cheaper fix than an image-error proxy, namely
per-site normalisation, together with the finding that this fix buys nothing on a
backbone whose sites are already balanced; and (iv) two negative results the lens
predicts (Secs.~\ref{sec:dagger} and \ref{sec:flux}). Precedent for the \emph{form} of
the fix is also clear: reweighting a diffusion MSE so no noise level dominates is the
P2 \cite{choi2022p2} and Min-SNR \cite{hang2023minsnr} argument, and normalised
relative $L_2$ is standard in operator-learning surrogates.

\paragraph{Distillation and solvers.} Distillation
\cite{salimans2022progressive,song2023consistency} and better solvers
\cite{lu2022dpmsolver} reduce step count rather than per-step cost, and are
complementary to caching. Unlike distillation, a corrector is trained post hoc on a
frozen model with cheap labels and leaves the sampler unchanged.

\paragraph{Exposure bias.} Training a corrector on states it will not encounter is
the classic exposure-bias problem \cite{bengio2015scheduled,ross2011dagger}. We
collect on-policy with respect to the cached rollout, and we test whether going
fully on-policy (DAgger) helps. It does not, for reasons that are specific and
measurable (Sec.~\ref{sec:dagger}).

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Cached unit.} Let the block stack have $B$ blocks and the sampler $T$
steps. Anchors are placed on a fixed interval $i$, i.e.\ at steps
$\{s : s \bmod i = 0\}$; all other steps are reuse steps. We cache the residual of
the \emph{entire stack} as a single unit, so a reuse step performs one injection.
This choice matters and is justified in Sec.~\ref{sec:mechanism}; we note here that
for correction-free caching, any contiguous partition of the stack into cached
segments is mathematically identical (the cached residuals simply sum), so the
partition changes only the corrector's injection structure, never the baseline. A unit
test verifies this to $10^{-6}$; it is \emph{not} bitwise, since the two orders sum
floating-point terms differently. It also makes the coarsest granularity strictly
cheaper: it removes $B$ per-block dispatches per reuse step, worth $10.5\%$ wall
clock on our primary backbone at $i{=}5$.

\paragraph{Corrector.} $C$ is a residual MLP over tokens with AdaLN conditioning on
$(t,\tau)$ and a zero-initialised output projection, so an untrained $C$ is exactly
plain caching. It is not purely token-wise: a single multi-head attention block is
inserted at mid-depth, which is where much of its per-call cost sits.
$C$ has $11.9$M parameters on the primary backbone and $17.8$M on the secondary
(the input/output projections scale with model width). Following a linear-probe diagnostic showing $\dR$ is largely a linear
function of the input drift, it also carries a low-rank linear path
$\dR \mathrel{+}= (xA)B$. Injection is damped by a scalar $\sigma$:
$h + R_a + \sigma \hat{\dR}$.

\paragraph{Backbones.} Primary: PixArt-$\Sigma$-XL-2-512 \cite{chen2024pixartsigma}
(0.6B, $B{=}28$, $T{=}20$, CFG 4.5, fp16, $512^2$). Secondary:
FLUX.1-dev \cite{flux2024} (12B, 19 dual-stream + 38 single-stream blocks, flow
matching \cite{lipman2023flowmatching}, $T{=}20$, $512^2$, bf16). Single NVIDIA A40.

\paragraph{Protocol.} Prompts are templated and split \emph{subject-disjointly} into
train ($112$ prompts / $28$ subjects), val ($24$ / $6$) and test ($24$ / $6$); no
subject appears in two splits. Only $6$ independent subjects in the held-out split is
a real constraint on inference and we treat it as one throughout. We report SSIM \cite{wang2004ssim}, LPIPS \cite{zhang2018lpips} and PSNR.
All image metrics are computed against the \emph{same-seed exact}
output, i.e.\ they measure fidelity to what the unaccelerated model would have
produced. The damping $\sigma$ is always selected on val and only then confirmed on
test. Cost is reported as FLOPs measured with \texttt{FlopCounterMode} on the real
pipeline call (VAE decode included) and, separately, as wall clock from an exclusive
benchmark that refuses to run if another process holds GPU memory.

\paragraph{What the cost numbers exclude.} Following the standard practice in this
line of work we precompute text embeddings, so reported cost excludes the T5 text
encoder. This is favourable to \emph{all} cached variants including the baselines,
but it should be stated plainly: on our primary backbone T5 is $173$\,ms of a
$1160$\,ms exact latency ($15\%$), and counting it end-to-end reduces the cacheable fraction
from $91.6\%$ to $78.1\%$ and the all-cache ceiling from $11.96\times$ to
$4.56\times$. The $\approx\!4\times$ operating points we study are therefore close to
the ceiling of the whole approach under single-image end-to-end accounting; the
amortised (many images per prompt) accounting is the one our numbers describe.
